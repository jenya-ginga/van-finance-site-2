Van Finance for Tree Surgeons and Arborists

Between chainsaws, chippers, climbing kit and a trailer to tow, an arborist's van has to work harder than most, and that's worth factoring in before signing any finance agreement.

Why tree surgeons need the right van

A typical day for a tree surgeon involves loading and unloading chainsaws, pole saws, climbing ropes and harnesses, PPE, first aid kit and fuel at one site, then doing it again at the next, sometimes several times before lunch. Kit needs to be secure, dry and organised rather than loose in a boot, which is why van choice for this trade is driven as much by what's fitted inside as by the size of the vehicle itself.

Most arborists run a medium to large panel van fitted out with internal racking, secured chainsaw storage and dedicated PPE compartments, sometimes in a crew cab layout to carry a small ground team. Towing capacity is just as important as load space for many businesses, since a wood chipper is often towed on a trailer behind the van – meaning gross train weight, not just the van's own payload, needs to be checked against the combination you actually plan to tow.

Demand for tree work is seasonal – storm-damage callouts, spring and summer pruning, and hedge-cutting windows can mean a busy few months followed by a much quieter winter. That pattern is relevant to how lenders assess affordability for a self-employed arborist: a full year of accounts or bank statements tends to give a fairer picture of the business than a snapshot taken during a quiet month, so it's worth having a full trading year ready to show.

Financing considerations for tree surgeons and arborists

Towing capacity is a genuinely different consideration for this trade compared with most other van-based businesses. Before financing a van, check its kerb weight and towing limit against the actual weight of a loaded chipper trailer, not just an empty one – under-specifying here can mean the van you've financed isn't legally able to tow what the job needs, which is a costly mistake to discover after the agreement is signed.

Seasonal income makes it worth stress-testing a repayment before committing to it. Running the numbers through an affordability calculator using a realistic quiet-month income, rather than a peak summer month, gives a more honest view of whether a monthly HP or PCP payment still fits comfortably in January as well as June.

Many arborists spend as much again on racking, chainsaw compartments and secure tool storage as they do on the base van, so it's worth asking upfront whether a finance quote covers just the vehicle or the vehicle plus that fit-out. This matters particularly on PCP, where the agreed future value is based on the van as financed – if the fit-out was added afterwards and isn't included in the agreement, it generally isn't reflected in that valuation either.

FAQ

Q: Can van finance include the cost of a chipper trailer or towing equipment?

A: Finance agreements are generally arranged against the van itself rather than a separate trailer, though some lenders may consider a fitted-out, ready-to-tow vehicle as part of the deal – it's worth asking each lender directly what the finance amount is being calculated against.

Q: How do lenders view seasonal income when I apply for van finance?

A: Most lenders assessing a self-employed arborist will look at income over a full trading year rather than a single recent month, so a busy summer and a quieter winter should even out in the assessment – having a full year of bank statements or accounts ready helps show that pattern clearly.

Q: Can I include racking and tool storage costs in my van finance agreement?

A: Some lenders will finance a van with fit-out and equipment storage already included, effectively lending against the ready-to-work vehicle rather than the bare van, though this varies by lender – check whether a quote is for the base vehicle only before comparing it with one that includes the fit-out.
